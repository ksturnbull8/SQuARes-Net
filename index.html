Research experience beyond local QI and audit
National scheme · NIHR

Associate Principal Investigator Scheme

A six-month, in-work training opportunity for anyone starting out in research. You work alongside the local Principal Investigator (PI) on an NIHR portfolio study at your own site, getting hands-on experience of how a multicentre study is actually delivered.

  • Mentored by the local PI for at least six months, working through a checklist of study activities and a learning pathway on NIHR Learn
  • Certified Associate PI status on completion, endorsed by the NIHR and Royal Colleges — a strong addition to your training portfolio
  • Open to doctors from FY1 upwards, plus nurses, midwives and AHPs; unfunded, so it runs alongside your usual clinical role
  • You'll need GCP training and your local PI's approval, and must register before you start — it can't be recognised retrospectively

The Scottish Intensive Care Society Audit Group (SICSAG) is part of Public Health Scotland's Scottish National Audit Programme, auditing the care and outcomes of ICU and HDU patients across Scotland.

Higher degrees: getting started

MDs and PhDs in anaesthesia

Every anaesthetist engages with research to some degree — at minimum by using evidence-based guidelines and protocols, and by meeting the research requirements of ARCP and CCT. At the other end of the spectrum, some anaesthetists move into full-time academic roles and stop practising clinically. Most people sit somewhere in between.

This section is about the more intensive end of that spectrum: higher degrees, meaning MDs and PhDs. These take a significant time commitment on top of standard training, and compared with clinical training the academic pathway can feel fairly opaque — so here's a brief overview.

An MD is typically two years full-time equivalent, done alongside clinical work. A PhD is typically three years full time, and project or lab based. You don't need a Master's or an intercalated degree first, though many people who go on to a higher degree have done one — often as a way of testing their interest in research.

— Dr Jack Cafferkey

Why people do one

Reasons vary, and you don't need a settled answer before you start exploring. Common ones include:

  • Testing whether a research career is for them
  • Knowing they want a clinical academic career, and using this as the route in
  • Gaining transferable skills, without necessarily planning to do research again
  • Keeping research as a small, ongoing part of a mainly clinical career
  • Wanting sustained project work rather than the pace of clinical practice
  • Wanting time in a non-clinical environment for personal reasons

How to start thinking about it

Talk to as many people as possible. This is the single most useful thing you can do. Speak to people in clinical and academic roles, in anaesthesia and outside it, and at different career stages. Most people's view of research is shaped by their own route into it, so hearing from several gives you a much fuller picture.

These conversations also tell you what a supervisor or programme is actually like to work with — not just what's on paper — and put you on people's radar for future opportunities.

They rarely happen by chance. Expect to send cold emails and book time with people you don't know well. It will feel awkward at first; that's normal.

Before those meetings

  • Have a think about why research interests you, even if the answer isn't fully formed
  • Read a little about what the person works on — their general area, not their results
  • Have a rough sense of your timeline: exams, training milestones, life events, and how much flexibility you have
  • Understand how PhDs tend to be funded (below) — it shapes what applying looks like

How PhDs tend to be funded

Project-based studentships

A principal investigator has grant funding that includes a PhD post, advertised on sites like Find a PhD. You apply to work on a predefined project. Less common for clinicians, more common for scientists.

Upside:
you know roughly what you're signing up for
Downside:
less freedom to shape the project

Doctoral training programmes

A funder gives a university or other body money to run a structured programme (e.g. ECAT, Wellcome PhD fellowships, multimorbidity CDTs), usually advertised on university or NHS jobs boards. Often designed with clinicians in mind.

Upside:
salaries closer to clinical ones, a cohort for peer support, and help moving in and out of training and negotiating with TPDs and rota coordinators

Personal fellowships

You develop a project with a supervisor and write your own grant application to fund it. Generally seen as the most prestigious route, and a strong training experience in itself.

Best suits:
someone with an established supervisor relationship, a specific question and some pilot data
Downside:
most competitive, and more of the admin and logistics falls on you

There are other, less common arrangements. Some private hospitals fund an MD in exchange for on-call commitments, and some people self-fund a PhD. Self-funding is worth thinking through carefully: you're paying fees and losing clinical income at the same time, and part-time PhDs carry their own challenges alongside clinical training — so get advice from several people before going down that route.
